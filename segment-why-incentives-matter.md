# Segment brief — Why incentives matter

**Slots into Longform 01 between segments 3 and 4.**
**On-camera question:** "Why do rewards actually change what people do?"
**Target:** 5 min (the longest segment — it's the thesis)
**Yields:** 3 shorts, including one for the merchant pillar

---

## Why this segment needs to exist

Right now the longform argues that loyalty in Kenya is *fragmented*. That's a structural argument, and it's true, but it skips a step: **it assumes the audience already believes rewards change behaviour.** Most people don't. Most people think loyalty programs are marketing noise — because the ones they've used were.

Without this segment, a sharp viewer (especially an investor) is left with an unanswered objection: *why is a rewards network a real business and not a gimmick?* This is the answer.

It also reframes Akiba's core value. Without it, one-account-everywhere sounds like **convenience**. With it, the network becomes the thing that makes incentives *work at all*. Convenience is a feature. That's a thesis.

---

## The argument, in order

### 1. People don't act on intentions. They act at the moment of payment.

Everyone intends to spend better. Almost nobody changes where they shop. The gap between intention and behaviour is where every good idea about money dies.

An incentive isn't a bribe — it's a **prompt at the exact moment a decision is being made.** That's why it works when advice doesn't. You can tell someone to shop smarter for a year and change nothing. Give them a reason at the till and they move.

> On camera: *"Kila mtu anataka kuspend better. Lakini hakuna mtu anabadilisha vile anashop. Hiyo gap ndio shida."*

### 2. Rewarding someone who already showed up is cheaper than convincing a stranger.

A merchant has two options. Pay to reach people who've never walked in, or give value to people already standing there. The second costs a fraction of the first per outcome.

Advertising buys **attention**. Incentives buy **behaviour**. Most small businesses in Kenya are spending on the first while ignoring the second, because the first is what's sold to them.

### 3. A discount is a transaction. An incentive is a loop.

This is the distinction most businesses never make.

A discount closes one sale and teaches the customer to wait for the next promo. You've bought revenue today and trained price-sensitivity for tomorrow. An incentive that *accrues* buys something different — a reason to return. You're not paying for a sale, you're paying for the next visit.

Same money out of the merchant's pocket. Completely different asset acquired.

### 4. Small, certain, and soon beats big, vague, and later.

Here's why most loyalty programs in Kenya fail, and it's not that people don't like rewards.

"Spend enough over a year and maybe get a voucher" is **large, uncertain, and delayed** — three properties that make an incentive behaviourally inert. Nobody restructures their week around a maybe. Meanwhile M-Pesa changed a country's behaviour partly on immediacy: you act, and something happens *now*, and you can see it.

Ask anyone in Nairobi about their Bonga points. They've been accumulating for years. Most people couldn't tell you the balance and have never spent them. That's not a failure of generosity — Safaricom gives real value. It's a failure of **design**: too slow, too abstract, too far from the moment of purchase.

> On camera: *"Bonga points — unajua balance yako? Exactly. Si ni kwamba hakuna value. Ni kwamba iko mbali sana na hiyo moment unalipa."*

### 5. An unredeemable reward is worse than no reward.

Points people can't spend don't just fail to work — they **actively teach distrust.** Every expired balance is a lesson that these programs are theatre. That's the real damage the last twenty years of Kenyan loyalty programs did, and it's the objection Akiba inherits before it says a word.

Which is why redeemability isn't a feature, it's the whole credibility test. *Kitu unaeza actually tumia.*

### 6. And this is why the network isn't convenience — it's the mechanism.

Now the pieces connect.

A single store's incentive is structurally weak. Your spend there is small and your visits are irregular, so any reward worth having is necessarily far away — which puts it straight back into "large, vague, later," the category that doesn't work. **A single merchant cannot design a good incentive.** The math forbids it.

Pool the same spending across many merchants and it inverts. Groceries, coffee, transport, airtime, hizo small small subscriptions — separately, each is too small to earn anything meaningful. Together, they're 20K a month, and the same unchanged behaviour now produces a reward that's **reachable, visible, and soon.**

That's the argument: the network isn't there to save you from carrying twelve cards. It's there because **incentives only function at scale, and no single shop has the scale.** Fragmentation isn't just annoying. It's what makes every individual program mathematically incapable of working.

> On camera: *"Store moja haiwezi build incentive nzuri. Si kwamba hawataki — ni math. Spend yako pale ni kidogo sana. Lakini ukiweka spend yako yote pamoja? Hiyo ni 20K. Hiyo inafanya kazi."*

### 7. Designed incentives steer, not just attract.

Once incentives are working, they become a steering wheel, not just a magnet. You can reward the specific behaviour a business needs: a second visit, a bigger basket, an off-peak visit, trying a merchant they've never used.

Cash back rewards nothing in particular. A designed incentive rewards the thing that actually grows the business.

---

## Say the counterargument out loud

Do not skip this. Naming the failure mode is what separates you from every founder who's over-claimed about loyalty, and it's the moment an investor decides you've thought about this properly.

**Incentives can attract exactly the wrong customer.** Reward discount-seeking hard enough and you assemble an audience that only appears when there's a promo and vanishes otherwise. That's not retention, it's a subsidy with extra steps — and plenty of loyalty programs have died that way.

**They can also crowd out the real reason someone came.** If a customer liked your coffee and you retrain them to come for the points, you've swapped a durable preference for a rented one.

Then give your answer: the point isn't maximum rewards, it's **rewarding the behaviour a merchant actually wants** — the repeat visit, the new-merchant trial — and keeping the reward small, certain, and soon rather than large and bribe-shaped. Incentive design is the product. That's the hard part, and it's the part you're building.

## One caution

This is the segment where you'll be most tempted to slip into lecture mode — it's the most conceptual material in the recording, and behavioural-economics framing pulls English-professional register.

Stay in your voice. Keep the Bonga points question in it. Every abstract point above has a concrete Kenyan example attached for exactly this reason — if you find yourself three sentences deep in theory with no example, cut back to one.
