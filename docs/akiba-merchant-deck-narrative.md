# Akiba Commercial Doctrine — Merchant Narrative

*Sprint 1 · Draft 2 · 24 August 2026*

**What this is:** the core commercial story, written once. The pitch deck is the primary artifact; the one-pager, landing page, sales script and outreach messaging are cuts of this same copy.

---

## The rule

> **Every commercial claim Akiba makes must fit into one of two buckets:**
>
> **1. Works today.**
> **2. Gets stronger as the network grows.**
>
> **Nothing in between.**

This rule exists because the failure mode of a marketplace business is overselling the network before it exists, and then losing a whole cohort in month four when the promise doesn't land. On a concentrated street, one merchant's disappointment is everyone's.

**The test that makes it enforceable:** *can you point to a merchant who got this last month?* If yes, it's bucket 1. If the honest answer is "not yet, but soon" — it's bucket 2, and it must be visibly labelled as such. A claim that sounds like bucket 1 but is really bucket 2 is the only kind of lie this deck can tell.

**The rule governs more than the deck.** It applies to the landing page, WhatsApp outreach, the one-pager, and — most importantly — what a field rep says out loud at the end of a slow month. That's where overselling actually happens. Build it into Sprint items 3 and 5.

**One extension.** Any claim about *another merchant's* results must be one you'd be willing to have the merchant verify by walking down the street. In a concentrated ward, they can and they will.

---

**Written for the cold start.** Every claim here is true in month one with an empty network in Mombasa.

# Slide 1 · The customer who bought from you this morning

> ### The customer who bought from you this morning will spend money eleven more times this week.

**Body:**

Tuktuk to work. Chai and mandazi. Lunch. Airtime. Something from the duka on the way home. Barber on Saturday. Medicine when the child is sick.

**KES 8,000 to 15,000 a month** — spending they cannot avoid.

**You see one of those moments. You don't see the other eleven.**

**Visual:** a single day laid out as a strip of small icons with amounts — the merchant's own shop marked once, in colour, everything else grey. The picture should make the point before anyone reads a word.

**Say this:** *"Think about the last customer who walked out of here. Where else did they spend money today?"*

# Slide 2 · None of it compounds

> ### They might come back tomorrow. They might not. You'll never know why.

**Body:**

- You don't know who your regulars are — you recognise faces, not patterns
- You don't know who stopped coming, or when
- You have no way to give a good customer a reason to choose you again
- The shop next door sells what you sell, at your price

Every sale starts from zero. Nothing builds. **None of it compounds.**

Right now the only things keeping a customer are habit and location. **Both of those can change tomorrow.**

*This is true whether business is good or bad.*

**Visual:** two identical shopfronts side by side, nothing to choose between them.

**Say this:** *"You know your good customers by face. But if one of them stopped coming last month, would you know?"*

> **Note.** "Compounds" is the doctrine word and it should recur — Akiba is a compounding layer over commerce. But it's an abstract financial term, and across a counter in Majengo it will not carry. The spoken and Swahili versions use the concrete equivalent: **nothing builds up.** Same idea, no translation gap.

# Slide 3 · Why this is finally possible

> ### Small businesses never did this before because it was too much work. That's no longer true.

**Body:**

Paper cards. Stamps. A book under the counter. Someone remembering to ask. It was never that shop owners didn't want to reward good customers — it's that the admin cost more than the customer was worth.

Four things changed:

| | |
|---|---|
| **93 of every 100 phones in Kenya are smartphones** | Your customers can all take part |
| **Almost everyone already pays by phone** | Nothing new for them to learn |
| **A code on your counter replaces the card, the stamp and the book** | Nothing new for you to manage |
| **The wallet is already on their phone** | Nothing to download |

**Visual:** old punch card, torn and faded, on the left. A counter code on the right. The four rows underneath.

**Say this:** *"The reason shops like yours never did this is that it used to be too much work. That's the only thing that's changed — but it changes everything."*

> **Note.** This is the reframe from "what's changed" — a slide about the world — to a slide about *the merchant's barrier disappearing*. Each supporting fact is tied to a specific piece of work that no longer exists. The last row plants MiniPay without spending it; slide 6 pays it off.

# Slide 4 · Introducing Akiba

> ### Akiba turns everyday spending into a reason to come back.

**Body:**

Your customers earn **Akiba Miles** when they spend with you. They spend those Miles on offers — yours, and other businesses in the network.

The money they were already spending starts bringing them back.

---

**Akiba is not:**

- **Not a payment system.** Your customer pays you exactly as they do now — cash, M-Pesa, however you take money today.
- **We never touch your money.** It goes straight to you, as it always has.
- **We take no percentage of your sales.** Not one shilling.
- **Miles are not a debt.** They are not money you owe anybody.

---

**Visual:** the "is not" list should be a visible box, not fine print. It is the most trust-building element in the deck.

**Say this:** *"Before I explain how it works — let me tell you what it isn't, because most people assume wrong."*

# Slide 5 · How it works

> ### Four steps. Nothing changes about how you take money.

**Body:**

1. **Your customer pays you** — cash, M-Pesa, exactly as normal
2. **They show you their Akiba code** on their phone
3. **You scan it and enter what they spent** — they earn 1 Mile per KES 100
4. **They spend Miles on offers** — including the ones you create

Miles build up across everywhere they shop. That's the point: the customer earns from spending they can't avoid, and spends those Miles on things they actually want.

**And they can find you.** Customers browsing Akiba for places to earn and spend see your business on the map.

**Visual:** four panels, photographed in a real Mombasa shop. Not illustrations. Frame 2 should show a customer holding up a phone — that's the whole ask on their side.

**Say this:** *"Nothing changes at your till. They show you a code, you scan it and put in the amount. Takes five seconds."*

> **Product note — not for the deck, but it changes the strategy.** The mechanic is **merchant-scans-customer**, and that inverts where the friction sits.
>
> On the consumer side this is close to ideal. Their entire action is "show your phone" — the same gesture as showing an M-Pesa confirmation. The habit problem we kept circling is much smaller than modelled: no app to open under time pressure, no scanner to find, no queue anxiety.
>
> **But the friction moved to your staff, and that's now the binding constraint.** Every single transaction requires a merchant-side action: pick up the device, open Akiba, scan, key in the amount, confirm. At a busy duka at 6pm, that gets skipped. Three consequences worth planning for:
>
> - **"Scan success rate" is now a merchant-compliance metric, not a consumer one.** The customer almost never fails; the shop does. Track it per merchant, and treat a falling rate as a churn signal — a merchant who stopped scanning has already left.
> - **Staff training is load-bearing, and "ten minutes once" understates it.** Staff turnover means retraining, and the person at the counter at 6pm is often not the owner who signed up.
> - **The merchant keys in the amount**, which is what powers the basket data behind slide 8's analytics promise — genuinely good. It also means data quality depends on merchant accuracy, and a merchant watching their Miles allowance has a quiet incentive to under-enter. Worth monitoring internally.
>
> One upside worth naming: because the merchant performs the action, they *experience* the network on every transaction. That's very good for belief, which we established is the real currency in Bridge 1.

# Slide 6 · This already works

> ### Akiba isn't an idea. 65,000 Kenyans already use it.

**Body:**

- **65,000+ users in Kenya**
- Built inside **MiniPay** — the wallet already on their phone. Nothing new to download.
- **30,000+ reward claims**, with no promotion running

---

**People are already forming the habit. 30,000 claims — with nothing on offer.**

**Visual:** three numbers, large, nothing else. The MiniPay logo.

**Say this:** *"Thirty thousand claims with no promotion running. That's not people responding to a campaign. That's a habit."*

> **Note on the wording.** You're right that "when nobody is paying them to" is abstract — but replacing it with "people are already forming reward habits" alone loses the thing that makes it land, which is that it's a fact rather than an assertion. Any startup can claim habits are forming. So: **claim first, proof immediately after, one line each.** The statement is what you want believed; the number is why they believe it.

> **Note on numbers.** Lead with 65,000 Kenya for Kenyan merchants — not the global figure. A merchant hearing a larger number will assume Kenyans, and if they later learn otherwise you've lost credibility on the one slide whose entire job is credibility. For institutional and government audiences the fuller shape works well: proven across multiple markets, 65,000 in Kenya.

**Body:**

**They earn on what they can't avoid:**
transport · the duka · lunch · the barber · the pharmacy · chai

**↓**

**Akiba Miles**

**↓**

**They spend them on what they want:**
a meal out · shoes · airtime · coffee · a day out

The reward asks nothing of them. It just makes the spending they were already doing worth something.

---

**That's the behaviour we're bringing to Mombasa.**

**Visual:** needs on the left in plain type, Miles in the middle, wants on the right in colour. Two arrows. Nothing else on the slide.

**Say this:** *"We're not asking anyone to spend more. We're asking them to spend in the same places they already do — just the ones where it counts for something."*

> **Note — accepted, with one condition attached.** You're right that the slide was doing two jobs and that stripping it back to needs → Miles → wants is cleaner. But moving the closing line from construction to aspiration means **slide 8 is now the only place in the deck that admits the network is still being built.** That admission is what makes everything else credible.
>
> So: **slide 8's line — "We're building the second part in your neighbourhood right now" — is now load-bearing and non-negotiable.** If someone later trims it for punchiness, this becomes an overselling deck and nobody will notice until month four. Worth writing that down somewhere it will be seen by whoever edits this next.

# Slide 8 · What you get

> ### Some of this works from day one. Some of it grows with the network. Here's which is which.

## From day one — whether the network is big or small

- **Campaign tools.** Create an offer, set the rules, see exactly who claims it. *"Visit 5 times, get 25% off"* — you decide.
- **Your customer data.** Who your regulars are. Who stopped coming. What they spend, and when.
- **Featured content.** We make videos about your business with a real offer attached — and you get the numbers that matter: claims, scans, first-time customers. Not views.
- **You're on the map.** Your business appears in Akiba when customers look for places to earn and spend.
- **Signage and materials**, provided.

## As the network grows in your area

- Customers choosing you **because they earn here**
- **Real traffic from that listing** — people with Miles to spend, looking for somewhere to spend them
- Position against the shop next door that isn't on Akiba

---

**We're building the second part in your neighbourhood right now. The first part starts the day you join.**

**Visual:** two clearly separated columns. The day-one column should look heavier and more concrete than the growth column — because it is.

**Say this:** *"I'm not going to promise you customers tomorrow. What I can tell you is exactly what you get tomorrow, and exactly what comes later."*

> **Do not edit the closing line.** See slide 7's note. This is the deck's only remaining statement that the network is under construction, and the doctrine rule depends on it.

# Slide 9 · One customer

> ### Imagine one customer.

**Body:**

Amina buys lunch from you most Tuesdays. KES 250. She holds up her phone, you scan it, she earns a couple of Miles. Neither of you thinks much about it.

She's earning at the tuktuk too. And the duka. And the pharmacy. Places she was going anyway.

You put up an offer: **visit five times, get KES 200 off your next meal.**

Five weeks later she claims it. She brings her sister.

---

**Here's what changed for you:**

- You know Amina exists — not as a face, as a customer
- You know she comes about once a week
- You know it was **your offer** that brought her in on that Thursday
- You know her sister is new, and where she came from

**She was an anonymous customer. Now she's someone you can actually do something about.**

**Visual:** no interface, no screenshots. A simple illustrated or photographed sequence. Five frames at most.

**Say this:** *"Let me tell you what this looks like for one customer."*

> **Note.** Placed after slide 8 deliberately — the payoff list only makes sense once the day-one benefits have been named. It also consolidates slide 8 emotionally before the ask sequence begins. For walk-ins, this slide is likely stronger than slide 8: across a counter in two minutes a story lands harder than a list. Slide 8 becomes the follow-up if they engage.

# Slide 10 · What we need from you

> ### This only works if it's visible.

**Body:**

- **Ask every customer** — "are you on Akiba?" Five seconds, every sale
- **Train whoever is at the counter** — including new staff. We'll come back and do it again whenever you hire
- **Display the card** where customers can see it — we provide it
- **Honour the offers you create** — you set them, so you control them
- **Tell us when something isn't working** — we'd rather hear it early

---

**That's it. No new till. No new system. No change to how you take money.**

**Visual:** a counter with the Akiba card visible next to the M-Pesa till number. Real photo.

**Say this:** *"There's work involved and I'd rather say so. The main thing is asking every customer — that's the part that makes it work or not."*

> **Note — this slide got more important with the merchant-scan mechanic.** Since the merchant performs the action, staff compliance is the single point of failure in the whole system. "Ten minutes once" was written for a customer-scan flow and understates it. The revised list leads with the behaviour that matters (asking every customer) and makes retraining an ongoing commitment from Akiba rather than a one-off — which is both honest and a genuine differentiator against anyone selling software and walking away.

> **Note on the Miles line.** "2,500 Miles" is gone, and it was right to cut it — a pricing slide is the worst place to introduce a unit the merchant has to mentally convert. # Slide 12 · Your first 90 days

> ### Here's exactly what happens after you sign.

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Setup. Your code printed and placed. Ten minutes with your staff. Your first campaign live. |
| **Week 1** | Your business featured in Akiba content, with a real offer attached. First customers claiming. |
| **Weeks 2–4** | First campaign results. You start seeing who your regulars actually are. |
| **Month 2** | Second campaign — this time built on your own customer data, not a guess. |
| **Month 3** | **Review results. Renew at KES 500. Plan the next campaign.** |

**Visual:** a simple timeline. Dates, not phases.

**Say this:** *"Most people who sell you something disappear after you pay. Here's what our next ninety days look like — including the part where you decide whether it was worth it."*

> **Note — and this goes further than making renewal visible.** If the introductory rate is KES 250 for three months, then **month 3 is the price step**, and the deck should name the number. Putting "Renew at KES 500" on the timeline in the first meeting means the step was agreed at signup rather than sprung at renewal.
>
> This is not a copy detail. Renewal is the single biggest risk in the first cohort, and a synchronised cliff is how a whole street churns at once. A merchant who saw KES 500 written on a slide in September is a fundamentally different renewal conversation in December than one who thought the price was KES 250.

# Open question the mechanic raises

Slide 10 now commits Akiba to ongoing staff retraining, which is the right promise but a real cost — a field visit every time a merchant hires. At 75 merchants with typical MSME staff turnover that's a recurring load nobody has budgeted.

Two cheaper substitutes worth testing before this scales: a one-page laminated counter card that teaches the flow without a visit, and a 60-second video the owner can show a new hire on WhatsApp. Neither replaces the first training. Both might replace the fifth.
