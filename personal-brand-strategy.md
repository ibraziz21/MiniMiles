# Ibrahim Aziz — Personal Brand Strategy

**Platforms:** TikTok + YouTube
**Time budget:** ~5 hrs/week
**Goals:** consumer awareness → merchant acquisition → investor pull
**Horizon:** 6 months

---

## 1. The core problem to solve first

You picked three goals that pull in different directions:

| Audience | Who they are | What makes them stop scrolling |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Kenyan, 18–35, value-conscious, mobile-first | "My normal shopping could be giving me more back" |
| Merchants | SME owners, 28–50, cash-flow anxious | "This brings customers in and brings them back" |
| Investors | Global, English-language, pattern-matching | "This founder has distribution and taste" |

The mistake most founders make is trying to serve all three in every video. Don't. **Serve consumers with volume, and let the other two audiences be a byproduct of doing that publicly and well.**

Here's why that works: an investor doesn't need you to make videos *for* investors. They need evidence you can acquire consumers cheaply. A merchant doesn't need a pitch deck. They need to see 40,000 Kenyans watching you talk about where to save money and think *I want to be on that list*. Your content is not a message to merchants and investors — it is the **proof** you show them.

So: **consumer-first content, founder-journey subtext.**

---

## 2. Positioning

> **Spend the same. Get more back.**

The full line: *the Kenyan who shows you how to get more out of money you're already spending — and is building the network that does it automatically.*

The frame is **accumulation, not defence.** You are not warning people they're being ripped off. You are showing them that the money already leaving their pocket every week — groceries, lunch, fuel, coffee — can come back as rewards, discounts, and better value, if they shop with a bit of intent. Same spend, more value.

Three things this frame buys you:

- **It's positive.** "You're overpaying" makes people feel stupid. "Your shopping can pay you back" makes them feel like they just found something.
- **It scales with Akiba.** A save-money brand caps out at coupons. An earn-and-get-more brand grows naturally into the network you're actually building — one account, rewards everywhere, value compounding across merchants.
- **It's the same promise merchants want to make.** A merchant can't say "shop here, you're being overcharged elsewhere." They *can* say "shop here and get something back." Your consumer content and your merchant pitch become the same sentence pointed in two directions.

Akiba means *savings* in Swahili — but read it as **accumulation**, not restraint. A store of value that grows. Your name, your company, and your content angle all point at the same idea. Very few founders get that alignment for free.

**What you are not:**
- Not a hustle-culture motivational account
- Not a "10 lessons from my startup journey" carousel guy
- Not a deals/discount aggregator page (those have no ceiling and no equity)
- Not the "you're being ripped off" guy — that's a different, smaller brand

**Tone:** calm, specific, generous, numbers on screen. You're the friend who did the math and is happy to share it.

**One honest trade-off:** loss framing ("you're overpaying") reliably out-hooks gain framing on short-form — outrage travels further than upside. You will feel this in the first month's numbers. Accept it. The fix is not to flip back to the negative frame; it's to make the gain **concrete and countable**, which is what the video concepts in §7 are built to do. "You could be getting X back" beats "here's a nice reward" every time.

## 3. Content pillars

Five pillars. The percentages are of total output, and they're weighted deliberately toward the top.

### Pillar 1 — More For Your Money (40%)
How to get more value back from spending you're already doing.

Examples: what your regular monthly shop could be earning you, stacking a reward on top of a discount, which everyday purchases give something back and which give nothing, where to get genuinely better value on the same item, what a reward is actually worth when you redeem it.

**Why:** highest reach ceiling, directly aligned with Akiba's consumer promise, and every video trains one habit — **check before you buy** — which is exactly the behaviour Akiba Pass needs.

**The discipline:** every video in this pillar ends with the viewer having *gained* something, not avoided something. Same facts, opposite emotional exit.

### Pillar 2 — Build in Public (25%)
The actual week. Merchant meetings, a feature that shipped, a number that moved, a decision you got wrong.

Examples: "We lost our third merchant this month — here's what they said", "This is what onboarding a merchant actually looks like", "We rebuilt the QR flow because nobody used it."

**Why:** this is the investor pillar and the loyalty pillar. It converts casual viewers into people rooting for you.

### Pillar 3 — Retention & Acquisition (15%)
Two questions, over and over: **how do you get a customer, and how do you get them back?** No stories, no motivational framing — just mechanics and math a shop owner can use on Monday.

Examples: "The cheapest customer you'll ever get is the one who already walked in", "Your discount got a first visit. What got the second?", "How to tell a regular from a walk-in without any software", "What a repeat customer is actually worth over a year."

**Why:** this is your merchant acquisition engine, and it's a direct demo of the value Akiba sells. A shop owner who learns retention from you will take your call about a retention product.

### Pillar 4 — Kenya Tech & Commerce Takes (15%)
Opinions on payments, M-Pesa, delivery apps, retail, and what's actually happening in African tech — from someone who builds.

**Why:** this is what gets you quoted, invited to podcasts, and noticed by investors and other founders. Highest per-video leverage, lowest volume.

### Pillar 5 — Personal / Origin (5%)
Why you left infrastructure work. What building in Nairobi costs you. The occasional face-to-camera about doubt or momentum.

**Why:** rationing this is what makes it land. Once a month, not once a week.

## 4. Platform strategy

These are not the same job. Treat them as such.

### TikTok — discovery engine
- **Length:** 25–45 seconds. Under 60 always.
- **Format:** face-to-camera, hard hook in the first 1.5 seconds, one idea per video, text on screen throughout (a large share of Kenyan viewing is sound-off in public).
- **Volume:** 4–5 posts/week. TikTok rewards frequency more than polish.
- **Pillar mix:** heavy on More For Your Money and Build in Public.
- **Comments are the goldmine.** Every "but what about X?" comment is your next video. Reply with video, not text.
- Post between 7–9pm EAT on weekdays. Test 1pm as a secondary slot.

### YouTube — trust and depth engine
Two tracks:

**Shorts** — repost your TikToks. Zero extra cost, different audience, and Kenya's YouTube base skews slightly older and more business-y. Strip the TikTok watermark.

**Long-form** — 8–14 min, **one per week, non-negotiable.** This is the asset that closes merchants and warms investors, because it's the thing someone watches after they've decided to take you seriously. Formats that work:
- *The Weekly Build* — what happened at Akiba this week, with real screens and real numbers
- *Merchant Visit* — go to a business, film the conversation, show the problem
- *The Breakdown* — 12 minutes on one topic (loyalty economics, why Kenyan retail struggles with retention, how M-Pesa changed pricing psychology)

Kenyan CPMs are low, so **don't build for ad revenue.** Build for the fact that a VC or a merchant will watch one full video and decide.

**Titles and thumbnails matter more than the video on YouTube.** Write the title first. If you can't write a title someone would click, don't shoot it.

## 5. The 5-hour week

The only version of this that survives contact with running a startup is **batching**. You do not film daily.

| Day | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| **Sunday** | 45 min | Plan: pick 5 short concepts + 1 long-form topic. Write hooks only, not scripts. |
| **Monday** | 90 min | **Batch shoot.** Same location, 2 shirt changes, film all 5 shorts back to back. |
| **Tuesday** | 60 min | Edit + caption shorts. Schedule all 5. |
| **Wednesday** | 90 min | Shoot + edit long-form YouTube. |
| **Daily** | 15 min | Reply to comments. Non-negotiable — this is the algorithm and the research. |

**Total: ~5 hours.**

### Rules that keep it at 5 hours
1. **Never film a video you haven't written a hook for.** Rambling is what turns 20-minute jobs into 2-hour ones.
2. **Phone only.** No camera, no crew, no lighting rig. Vertical, natural light, clip-on mic (~KES 2,000 — the single highest-ROI purchase; bad audio kills retention faster than bad video).
3. **One take, move on.** If it's not right by take three, the hook is wrong, not the delivery.
4. **CapCut for shorts, nothing fancier.** Auto-captions, then fix the Swahili and the proper nouns.
5. **The real work is the source material.** You already spend your week in merchant meetings and product decisions. Keep a running note on your phone: every time something surprises you, that's a video. You are not creating content — you are recording a job you already do.

### Batching cheat: film 5 in 90 minutes
Set up once. Say the hook, deliver the point, stop. No intro, no "hey guys", no outro. Change shirt after every two. Nobody notices the same wall; everybody notices the same shirt.

## 6. The hook is 80% of the work

Your first sentence has to do one of four things:

1. **Name a number** — "You spend 20,000 a month. Here's what that should be earning you."
2. **Contradict** — "Twelve loyalty cards is why you've never redeemed anything."
3. **Confess** — "We lost a merchant this week and it was my fault."
4. **Promise specificity** — "Three everyday purchases that give something back, and three that give you nothing."

Note the shape of 1 and 4: the number and the specificity carry the punch, so the video hits hard *without* telling the viewer they've been foolish. That's the whole positioning in one sentence structure.

**Never** open with: "Hi, I'm Ibrahim, founder of Akiba…" — that's an ad, and viewers are trained to swipe past it. Identity goes at the *end*, once you've earned three seconds.

**The CTA problem:** don't say "download Akiba" on every video. Once you're a commercial, retention collapses. Mention the product on roughly 1 in 4 videos, and only when the video's topic makes it obvious. Bio link does the rest of the work.

## 8. First 30 days

**Week 1 — Set up**
Clean up TikTok and YouTube profiles. Bio: *"Building Akiba — your everyday shopping, worth more."* One link. Buy the clip-on mic. Film your first batch of 5. Do **not** announce that you're starting a content journey — just start posting.

**Week 2 — Ship**
5 shorts + first long-form (#1, the origin video). Pin it. Reply to every single comment.

**Week 3 — Read the data**
Look at retention curves, not views. Where do people drop? Usually second 3. Which pillar over-indexed? Double down on it next batch — the audience is telling you what your positioning actually is, and it may not be what you guessed.

**Week 4 — Compound**
Make videos from your best comments. Reach out to 2–3 Kenyan creators in finance/business for a collab or duet. Send your long-form video to three merchants and watch whether it changes the conversation.

## 9. What to actually measure

**Ignore:** follower count, likes, views on any single video.

**Track weekly:**

| Metric | Why it matters | 6-month target |
|---|---|---|
| Average watch % on shorts | The only real quality signal | >50% |
| Comments per video | Intent, and your content pipeline | >20 |
| Bio link clicks → Akiba signups | Consumer awareness goal, measured | 500 attributed signups |
| Merchant inbound ("saw your video") | Merchant acquisition goal, measured | 10 inbound conversations |
| Long-form videos published | The only input you fully control | 24 of 26 |

That last row is the one that matters most. **Consistency beats every other variable.** A mediocre video published weekly for six months outperforms a brilliant one published sporadically — because the algorithm rewards the schedule, and because merchants and investors are watching whether you finish things.

---

## 10. The three traps

1. **Becoming a deals account.** If More For Your Money drifts into pure discount-posting, you'll grow fast and build nothing transferable. Every value video needs a *point of view*, not just a price. The test: would this video still be worth watching if the specific offer expired?

   **The sibling trap:** drifting back into "you're being ripped off" because it hooks harder. It will hook harder. It also caps you at outrage-bait and puts you in an adversarial position toward the merchants you need to sign. Watch for it in your own scripts.
2. **Making content for investors.** The moment you start posting for VCs, consumers can smell it and both audiences leave. Investors find you through consumer traction, not through founder-thought-leadership.
3. **Letting content eat the company.** Five hours is the ceiling, not the floor. If a week gets consumed by fundraising or a launch, publish the backlog and skip the shoot. The batch system exists so one bad week doesn't break the streak.
